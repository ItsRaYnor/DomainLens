## Tables no longer squeeze a column to a letter per line

On **Monitoring → Domain changes** the **Change** and **By** columns could be pressed to a
few letters wide ("Re / gi / str / y"), while **When** and **Domain** had room to spare. The
same could happen in other lists with a header row.

Two style rules met there. Every header cell of a table was given 28% of the width -- meant
for a label beside its value -- so in a list of six columns the first four took the whole
table. And a cell was allowed to break between any two letters, which let the remaining
columns shrink to a single character.

Now a header takes the width its column needs, text breaks between words (and inside one
only when that word alone is too long), and on a screen wider than a phone no column goes
below the width of a short word. In the domain change log a domain or a name server is kept
on one line. A list wider than its card scrolls sideways instead of running out of it. On a
phone, lists still turn into cards as before.

## The scan result's overview leads to its details

The tiles under **Security overview** lifted on hover like buttons but did nothing when clicked.
Below them the tabs sorted the same checks again, by another scheme: each group stacked its
tabs in a column under its own label, so the labels stood at different heights and a group of
one said its name twice ("EMAIL SECURITY / Email security").

Now the overview and the tabs read alike:

- The tiles are grouped under the same names as the tabs: Registration & DNS, Email security,
  Transport & web, Platform, External signals. Small groups sit side by side.
- A tile opens its details: the tab they are in, scrolled to that check's card, which lights up
  briefly. **HSTS** opens SSL/TLS at TLS Features, **DKIM** opens Email security at DKIM.
- The tabs are one row, with a thin line between the groups. The group names are kept for
  screen readers.

## Advice shows what the scan measured

An advice item said what to change, not what the scan had seen. "Weak cipher suites accepted"
said the weak ones "include RC4, 3DES, DES, NULL or EXPORT" when the suites found were CBC
suites with SHA-1. To check it you went to the SSL/TLS tab, where the same suites stood in two
tables with verdicts that disagreed. Above the advice list, every urgent item was printed a
second time, fix and retest included.

- Each item now has **What the scan measured**: the lines it rests on, such as the suites by
  name, the header and the URL it was read from, or the record as found. A button opens the
  card with the whole measurement. **Raw output of the check** shows the check's output as JSON.
- Each card in the tabs ends with **Advice from this measurement**, linking back to those items.
- The list at the top is now an index of links into the items, not a second copy.
- **SSL/TLS** has one cipher table with both verdicts. Under each suite it names the part the
  NCSC rates down (RSA key exchange, SHA-1 hash). The cipher order sits above it. The protocol
  cards show the NCSC level. The NCSC card keeps its verdict without repeating the advice.
- The weak-cipher advice now says what "weak" means here, without listing ciphers the scan did
  not find. "8 security headers missing" lists the 8 headers it counts.

A stored scan gets this when it is opened: its advice is drawn again from the stored
measurement, as its rating already was.

## Mail tiles for a domain without mail

A parked domain set up as it should be had red DKIM, MTA-STS and TLS-RPT tiles, and was told
to publish MTA-STS and TLS-RPT. That parked domain has a null MX, `v=spf1 -all`, DMARC reject
and revoked DKIM keys. The DKIM tile had no advice item at all to explain the red.

- With a null MX, the MTA-STS and TLS-RPT tiles read **Not applicable** and give the reason.
  The advice to publish them is gone: they protect inbound mail, and none arrives.
- Revoked DKIM keys on a domain whose SPF is `v=spf1 -all` are **Not applicable**.
- Only revoked keys on a domain that does send mail read **Context**, not Fail: the selector in
  use may not be one that was tried.
- No key found under the selectors tried reads **Not tested**, as the advice already said.

A domain that receives mail gets the same tiles and advice as before.

## No T grade for a certificate the scanner could not verify

Behind TLS inspection, for example a firewall on the container's network, every scanned site
got TLS grade **T**, a critical "Poor TLS grade (T)" and a red **SSL Valid** tile. The
certificate that answered belonged to the inspection device, chained to a root the container
does not have. The scan recorded only "not trusted" and dropped OpenSSL's reason.

Now the reason decides:

- **Expired, not yet valid, self-signed, or issued for another name**: faults of the
  certificate itself, wherever the scanner is. These stay **T**, with the reason.
- **A chain the scanner could not trace to a trusted root**: not measured. There is no grade,
  the TLS Grade and SSL Valid tiles say **Not tested**, and the advice is an info item. It names
  who issued the certificate that answered. The card shows that certificate. The item explains
  how to tell TLS inspection, a scanner without CA certificates and a server that leaves out its
  intermediate certificate apart.

Scans saved earlier keep the grade they were given.

## A cold scan takes seconds, not a minute

A scan of a domain that had not been scanned that day took 45 to 67 seconds, nearly all of it
waiting for two overloaded outside services: crt.sh (certificate transparency) and the Internet
Archive's Wayback Machine. Measured in the release image on three public sites, the same scan
now takes 6 to 34 seconds; how long depends on how crt.sh is doing that day. Nothing that is
measured changed with it, except where said below.

- **Wayback Machine.** The archive is asked for the homepage's captures, not for every capture
  of every page of the domain. That answers in about a second instead of 5 to 60 seconds or a
  503, and the homepage rows came first anyway. A query that times out is not asked a second
  time; the budget is 15 seconds instead of 30.
- **Wayback "last seen" is the last capture.** It was the 15th capture ever made, often years
  old: the archive was asked for the oldest days. The snapshots listed are now the 15 most
  recent days, and "first seen" is asked for separately. When that second question goes
  unanswered, first seen stays empty and says why.
- **crt.sh is asked once per scan.** The OSINT tab, takeover enumeration and the CAA check each
  asked crt.sh for the same list, with their own retries: up to nine requests per scan during an
  outage, and tabs that disagreed about the same certificates. Lookups of one domain at the same
  moment now share one request. OSINT waits 20 seconds per try for crt.sh, as the Security
  audit does; at 12 it gave up on answers that came after 14 to 17.
- **crt.sh is not asked again after a timeout.** A query that did not answer within 20 seconds
  is crt.sh overloaded on it; three tries took a scan past a minute. A fast failure (502, 504, a
  dropped connection) is still tried again. The last known list is shown, with its date, as
  before.
- **OSINT feeds are kept one by one.** The OSINT result was kept for the day only when every feed
  had answered, so one feed that was down sent all seven out again on the next scan. Each
  feed's answer is now kept on its own (a reused one says how old it is), and a forced scan asks
  all of them again. A crt.sh outage is no longer kept for the day in the OSINT result.
- **Side by side.** The DKIM selectors (about thirty DNS questions), the TLS signature-hash
  probes (up to five openssl handshakes per address) and the CORS, OPTIONS and soft-404 requests
  of the web-exposure check no longer wait for each other. The results are read back in the
  same order.
- **One CA bundle load.** Every new HTTPS connection loaded the certificate bundle from disk
  again: about 70 ms of CPU in the image, more on a slow NAS. It is loaded once now.
  Certificates are checked exactly as before; a request with its own bundle
  (`REQUESTS_CA_BUNDLE`) or with verification off is left as it was.

## Known CVEs of the version a site shows (opt-in)

**Admin → Settings → CVE data (NVD, NCSC-NL)** switches on a comparison of the version a site
shows with every known CVE of PHP, nginx, the Apache HTTP Server, lighttpd, OpenSSL, WordPress
and Drupal, from the National Vulnerability Database. It is off by default, because switching it
on sends requests to nvd.nist.gov (and advisories.ncsc.nl): once a day, and never twice within
two hours. No mirror is downloaded; only the CVEs of these seven products are kept. An NVD API
key (Admin → Settings → API keys, `NVD_API_KEY`) makes the sync faster; without one it pauses six
seconds between requests, as the NVD asks.

A scan then gives one finding per product, not one per CVE: "PHP/8.1.5: 31 known CVE(s), 2
exploited", with the worst score as its severity (an exploited CVE in CISA's KEV list makes it
at least high), the CVEs as evidence and the release to move to when every CVE has a fix in the
branch shown. The answers are kept apart:

- **vulnerable**: the version is inside the affected range of at least one CVE;
- **no known CVEs**: the data is current and no CVE the NVD has analysed names this version (no
  finding; CVEs the NVD has not analysed yet have no ranges and are not included);
- **cannot tell**: a distribution build (`Apache/2.4.52 (Ubuntu)`), a version without its patch
  release, or a CVE that applies only on some platforms -- an info finding with what to check
  on the server. Also a product that is in use with its version hidden (PHP from a `PHPSESSID`
  cookie or a `.php` address, WordPress from its cookies or its own paths), or with only its
  major version (`Drupal 11`): nothing to compare, so no finding;
- **not measured**: a version was shown, but the product's data is older than the setting
  allows (3 days by default) or was never fetched -- an info finding, never "no known CVEs".

OpenSSL is read from the Server header (`Apache/2.4.62 OpenSSL/3.0.13`). Its patch letters are
releases (`1.1.1f` is older than `1.1.1w`), also in the NVD's data. Named beside a distribution
build in the same header (`Apache/2.4.41 (Ubuntu) OpenSSL/1.1.1f`) it is that distribution's
package, and so is a `-fips` build: "cannot tell".

Where it shows:

- **Tools → Versions** lists **Known CVEs (NVD)** under **Checked for**: the products, how many
  CVEs are kept and when they were synced, or that it is switched off. A table shows per product
  the CVEs kept (and how many the NVD lists without versions), how many are exploited, the newest
  CVE, and per branch the release from which none of the known CVEs applies. A branch where a
  later release or an unfixed CVE is affected gets no such release, so nothing is offered as safe
  that is not. Each product seen also gets a line under **Why** and in the CSV.
- The scan's **Info Disclosure** card has **Known vulnerabilities**: every rule whose software was
  seen and the comparison per product, findings or not, or that it is switched off.
- **Admin → Vulnerability rules** shows per product how many CVEs are kept, when they were synced
  and the last problem, with **Sync now**.

Switched off, nothing at all is said about CVEs. The built-in and own vulnerability rules work
as before, beside it.

With **NCSC-NL** on (the default once the CVE data is on), the NCSC's advisories are read too:
a CVE finding names the NCSC advisory about its CVEs with its likelihood and damage, and
**Admin → Vulnerability rules** lists recent NCSC advisories about the seven products. **Use as
start** fills in a rule with the advisory's CVEs (only those the NVD lists for the product) and
link, and from the NVD data the release with the fix per branch and the release the flaw came in
with; the advisory itself gives no versions to compare with. Where the NVD has no fixed release,
the list says so and the admin adds them. An "HTTP Server" counts as Apache's only under vendor
Apache, so Oracle's own is not offered. Every advisory is checked against its published SHA-512
before it is used.

This product uses data from the NVD API but is not endorsed or certified by the NVD.

## New CVEs for monitored sites, without a new scan

With the CVE data on, the versions each active monitor showed at its last scan are compared again
after every sync, so a CVE published the day after a scan does not wait for the next one:

- a CVE that now applies to that version and did not at the scan becomes a **New known CVE**
  monitor event, such as "2 new known CVE(s) for PHP/8.1.5 on example.org: …";
- a CVE that already applied and is now listed as exploited (KEV) does too: "Now exploited
  (CISA KEV) for …".

The event goes out through the notification channels and ServiceNow like any other, subject to
the minimum severity set for notifications. Its severity is the worst score among those CVEs
(exploited makes it at least high), and it links to the scan that showed the version. What a
scan reported is not reported again, and a CVE once reported is not repeated at every sync. Only
measured versions count: a hidden version, a distribution build or product data that is not
current gives no event. **Tools → Versions** says when the last comparison ran and how many
events it made.

## Version checks read more of the page

These apply to the vulnerability rules (also the ones you wrote) as much as to the CVE data.

- **The whole page and every generator tag.** Only the first 20,000 characters were read, and
  only the first generator tag. A theme's inline CSS could push the tag out of reach, and a
  plugin's tag ("WordPress Download Manager 3.3.74") could come first; the WordPress rule then
  said nothing, as if WordPress were not there.
- **WordPress and Drupal without their generator tag.** A rule with the token `WordPress` or
  `Drupal` counts the software as in use when the page links its own paths on the same site
  (`/wp-content/`, `/wp-includes/`; `/sites/default/files/`, `drupal.js`): "cannot tell from
  outside" instead of silence. Links to those paths on another site do not count.
- **WordPress's version from its own core files**, when the generator tag is gone:
  `/wp-includes/css/dist/block-library/style.min.css?ver=6.8.1` and the emoji script. jQuery and
  plugins under the same paths have their own numbers and are not read. The answer says which
  file the version came from. The generator tag still comes first.
- **"WordPress 6.8" is a release**: WordPress names its x.y.0 release that way. It was "cannot
  tell, no patch release"; it is compared now.
- **A major version alone** (`Drupal 11 (https://www.drupal.org)`; Drupal 8 and later show no
  more) is named as such, "shows only its major version, not the release", instead of "the
  version is not shown" beside a column that showed it. It stays "cannot tell".
- On a phone the site list no longer makes the Versions page wider than the screen.

## Settings hold after a restart

The app read its settings once at start, before the database was attached, and kept that copy
until an admin saved something. Every restart therefore set **Admin → Settings** back to the
defaults and the configuration file, the CVE switch included, until the next save. The database's
settings now count from the start.

### Upgrading

Nothing to do. If DomainLens runs behind TLS inspection, the TLS tab of a new scan now says
so instead of grading T; scan from a network without inspection to measure TLS. Reload the page
once if the browser still shows the old layout.

The CVE data stays off until an admin switches it on; seven tables are created on first start.
When it is switched on, the first comparison of monitored versions takes stock silently of what
the monitors' last scans showed; **New known CVE** events start with what comes after.

For integrations: the scan result's `info_disclosure` has a new `cves` key (`null` while switched
off), and monitor events, webhooks included, have a new `event_type`, `known_cve_new`.

Settings saved under **Admin → Settings** now hold after a restart. If one was saved while a
restart had reset it, check that section once after upgrading.
